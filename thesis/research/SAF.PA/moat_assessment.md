## Deep Dive: Each Moat Source

### 1. Engine Duopoly (Cost Advantage + Efficient Scale)

**The structure:** CFM International is a 50/50 JV between Safran Aircraft Engines and GE Aerospace. It is one of only TWO suppliers of narrow-body commercial jet engines globally (the other being Pratt & Whitney, owned by RTX).

**Why it's a duopoly, not a competitive market:**
- Developing a new jet engine costs $10-20 billion and takes 10-15 years
- FAA/EASA certification is extraordinarily rigorous and time-consuming
- Airlines and OEMs require decades of reliability data before adoption
- Supply chain relationships are built over generations
- Only two aircraft OEMs matter (Airbus, Boeing) and both offer CFM engines

**Market share evidence:**
- CFM LEAP powers 75% of A320neo orders (selected by airlines over P&W GTF)
- LEAP is the SOLE engine option on the Boeing 737 MAX
- P&W GTF has suffered significant reliability issues (powder metal contamination forcing fleet-wide inspections), shifting orders toward LEAP
- CFM delivered 1,802 LEAP engines in 2025 (+28% YoY); P&W GTF deliveries declined slightly

**Durability: >25 years.** The LEAP will power narrow-body aircraft well into the 2040s. No successor engine is in development. The next-generation engine (RISE, also by CFM) targets 2035+ entry into service, further extending Safran's position.

### 2. Switching Costs (EXTREME)

**The mechanism is structural and multi-layered:**

1. **Aircraft-engine certification:** Once an engine is certified on an aircraft type (LEAP-1A on A320neo, LEAP-1B on 737 MAX), the combination is locked for the aircraft's production life (20+ years).

2. **Fleet commonality:** Airlines operating CFM56/LEAP fleets have training, tooling, spare parts inventories, and maintenance contracts aligned to CFM. Switching to P&W would require parallel investments worth hundreds of millions.

3. **Aftermarket contracts (RPFH):** Rate Per Flight Hour contracts lock airlines into CFM/Safran maintenance for the life of the engine (20-30 years). These contracts are the CORE of Safran's aftermarket revenue stream.

4. **OEM selection persistence:** Boeing exclusively offers LEAP on the 737 MAX. Airlines buying 737 MAX have NO CHOICE but CFM. On the A320neo, while P&W GTF is an option, CFM LEAP has captured ~75% share partly due to GTF reliability concerns.

**Quantitative evidence:**
- CFM56 shop visits at peak levels (2,300-2,400/yr through 2028) despite engine being decades old = customers locked in
- Aftermarket spare parts revenue for civil engines grew +17.6% in 2025 (CFM56-driven)
- LEAP aftermarket tripling 2024-2028 as installed base grows

### 3. Intangible Assets (Patents + Certification + Brand)

**Patents:** 16,000+ patents globally covering engine design, materials science (ceramic matrix composites, additive manufacturing), and systems integration. These protect specific design choices and manufacturing processes that are NOT replicable.

**Certification barriers:** This is possibly the most underrated moat source.
- Certifying a new jet engine type requires 5-10 years of testing and $10B+ investment
- Regulatory requirements (EASA, FAA) have become MORE stringent post-737 MAX crisis
- The CJ-1000A (China's attempt) has been in development since ~2010 and is not expected for mass production until ~2030 at earliest
- Even if certified, the CJ-1000A will likely be limited to domestic Chinese market initially

**Brand/Reputation:** CFM56 is the most successful commercial jet engine in aviation history. This reputation matters when airlines are selecting engines for $100M+ aircraft orders.

### 4. Landing Gear & Equipment (Secondary Moat)

Safran Landing Systems is the global #1 in aircraft landing gear with ~20% market share, operating across 80+ civil and military programs. Key positions:
- Sole or primary supplier on many Airbus and Boeing platforms
- 35,000 aircraft equipped
- Acquired Collins Aerospace's flight control business (2025) for $1.8B, now controlling ~25% of the $11.2B flight control market

**This is a SUPPORTING moat, not primary.** The landing gear market is less concentrated than engines, but still has very high barriers (certification, testing, safety requirements).

## Escenarios de Erosion

### 1. Most Probable Erosion Scenario: Gradual Chinese Market Loss (15-20yr horizon)
China develops CJ-1000A to adequate reliability by 2032-2035, re-engines domestic C919 fleet, and gradually reduces LEAP orders for Chinese airlines. Impact: Safran loses ~5-10% of addressable market over 15 years. Moat remains WIDE for the other 90%+ of global market. Probability: 40-50% over 15 years.

### 2. Tail Risk Scenario: GTF Reliability Breakthrough + Next-Gen Competition (10-15yr)
P&W solves GTF durability issues entirely, reclaims A320neo market share to 50/50, AND a radical new propulsion technology (not from CFM) emerges. Impact: Safran's propulsion margins compress from 23% to 15-18%. Still profitable, but moat narrows. Probability: 10-15% over 15 years.

### 3. Black Swan: Regulatory Grounding of LEAP (low probability)
A catastrophic LEAP engine failure leads to fleet-wide grounding (similar to 737 MAX scenario but for the engine). Impact: Severe but temporary. CFM56 experienced no such event in 40+ years. Probability: <2%.

## Conclusion

Safran possesses a **WIDE moat** supported by 4 of 5 moat sources, with the CFM International engine duopoly being one of the most durable competitive advantages in global industry. The combination of:

- A structural duopoly in the most critical aircraft component
- EXTREME switching costs (20-30 year lock-in per engine installed)
- Massive and growing installed base (35,000+ CFM56, ~6,000+ LEAP)
- Regulatory certification barriers that take 10-15 years and $10B+ to overcome
- 16,000+ patents protecting design and manufacturing IP
- Net cash balance sheet with 17.4% insider ownership

...creates a competitive position that is virtually unassailable for the next 20+ years. The only realistic threat (CJ-1000A) is limited to the Chinese domestic market and is 5-10 years away from mass production.

The moat is **strengthening**, not weakening: the LEAP installed base is growing rapidly (1,802 engines delivered in 2025 alone), creating decades of high-margin aftermarket revenue. P&W GTF reliability issues are shifting further share toward LEAP. The next-generation engine (RISE/CFM) ensures Safran stays at the frontier.
